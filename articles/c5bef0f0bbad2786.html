Nvidia played a key role enabling the AI gold rush thanks in large part to its recently ousted CEO Jensen Huang. Huang was a master of foreseeing technological changes, investing in chips to train AI earlier than most.

But just as he’s leaving, the company has a growing list of competitors with a range of new, advanced technology breathing down its neck. For Nvidia, where CFO Colette Kress has now taken the reins, the timing isn’t great.

“Everything sold today was designed two-to-three years ago,” says the CEO of a large AI company, who spoke to WIRED on condition of anonymity to avoid hurting business relationships. Kress, or whoever replaces her permanently, will have to chart the course that the chip industry will take two years from now, the CEO says.

Already, competitors have started to move in.

Nvidia dominates the market for AI accelerators, capturing 80 percent of sales, according to Silicon Analysis. Longtime nemesis AMD is now making a concerted effort to build competitive AI chips. Its share of the market has crept up from just two percent in 2022 to between 7 and 10 percent in 2026.

Big US cloud players—Google, Amazon, and Microsoft—are all developing more specialized chips to power the training and serving of AI models. Each has seen similar gains. These companies are designing their own silicon to reduce costs and to gain some independence from Nvidia.

Several startups now offer more specialized chips that promise improvements over Nvidia’s core products. Cerebras, for instance, sells giant ones meant to power giant AI models. AMD recently bought Taalas, a company that burns models’ weights into silicon to make computations ultra efficient. Using these chips requires rewriting a lot of code, however, to make them work with cutting-edge AI models.

Patrick Moorhead, chief analyst at MoorInStrat, a company that tracks the chip industry, says the rise of AI agents makes inference, or running models that have already been trained, more important. That’s an area where Nvidia has less of an edge compared to cloud hyperscalers and startups. Nvidia did, however, acquire a startup making specialized chips, called Grok, in December of 2025.

And because of the rise in AI assistants and agents, there’s also growing demand for general purpose central processing units (CPUs), Moorehead said, an area where Nvidia barely competes.

Nvidia still maintains a huge lead in AI thanks to Huang’s foresight. Huang cofounded the company in 1993 to design graphics chips for PCs. In the mid-2010s, he saw the promise of AI. He funded academic research on the topic, developed software to support AI engineering, and gave hardware to labs and startups.

In 2022, ChatGPT took AI mainstream and sales of Nvidia chips skyrocketed. The company’s revenue went from $26.91 billion in January 2022 to $215.94 billion in January 2026. The challenges it faces may not be existential—but they do add up, especially on a global scale.

China Challenge

While Nvidia’s fortunes have risen in the US, they have declined in the world’s other big market for AI, China. Washington is trying to restrict access to its most advanced chips, while Beijing is trying to develop its own chip industry. Nvidia is not allowed to sell its most powerful chips in China, but it can sell previous generations. Sales of these chips as well as non AI chips doubled over the past year, but China now accounts for only eight percent of the company’s overall revenue, compared with 20 percent in 2024, according to its fillings. It remains unclear how many chips exported to other countries may either end up in China or end up being used remotely by Chinese firms.

Huawei, a Chinese tech giant targeted with US sanctions for several years, has developed new kinds of supercomputers that harness many more chips than usual to train and run AI models. Some Chinese AI companies, such as Z.ai, which makes one of the world’s best open source models, say they’ve trained their models entirely using Huawei’s chips. If Chinese open-weight models gain traction in other countries, it could create a market for Chinese chips elsewhere.

Huang’s dealings with China played a major role in his downfall. He had a romantic relationship with Huang Le, also known as Alice Huang, a Chinese executive at a Singaporean company called Megaspeed who the US government suspects of being an asset of China’s Ministry of State Security. Megaspeed and its subsidiaries have reportedly imported between $4.6 and $7.5 billion Nvidia chips into southeast Asia. Reports suggest that the company may have provided access to the chips to Chinese AI firms.

Babak Minovi, a senior analyst at the Eurasia Group, a consultancy that focuses on technology and geopolitics, says Huang was right to point out that cutting off access to China would empower domestic rivals. “Huang was taking a pragmatic line, and he knows competition,” he says.

Trend Spotting

Even in markets that it dominates, Nvidia may turn out to be a victim of its own success. The AI models built using Nvidia’s chip are automating the very elements of coding, data analysis, and scientific research that help design chips.

Most chip design firms use specialized AI models to arrange components on processors—something that traditionally required deep engineering expertise and experience. And both OpenAI and Anthropic have recently touted the ability of their newest models to design electronics from scratch.

Moorhead says the shift towards AI chip design may not pose a huge threat to Nvidia because it’s riding the same wave.“They have access to AI too,” he notes.

But Moorhead says the way AI models can now quickly rewrite code to run on different hardware may prove a problem. For years, Nvidia has attracted developers by providing software that makes it easy to write programs and build models for its silicon. Its Compute Unified Device Architecture, or CUDA, has long been seen as a moat to ensure AI companies continue to rely on Nvidia chips.

But AI models are now so good at reading and writing code that they can port an AI model over to a competitor's silicon at speed.

Wafer, a startup that uses AI to port models to AMD and then serves them more cheaply, has seen revenue grow spectacularly in recent months, boasts CEO Emilio Andere.

Every time a major new open weight model is released Wafer can have it running on AMD hardware at least 80 percent as efficiently within hours, he says. Andere says AI is getting so capable that his engineers barely have to edit or review the code.

Dan Hutchinson, a veteran chip analyst, says Nvidia may be entering its “Intel swan moment,” referring to the 2019 replacement of engineering-focused CEO Brian Krzanich with its CFO Bob Swan. (Sound familiar? Krzanich also resigned after a relationship with an employee; Huang is alleged to have had multiple.) From there, the company’s focus shifted to sales more than technical progress. Bureaucracy ballooned and revenue tanked.

“Nvidia is now an Intel waiting to happen,” he says.
