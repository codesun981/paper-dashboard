A new approach known as “reverse game theory” aims to control powerful AI models by designing systems that incentivize them to stick to the rules.

As AI models become more capable, safety researchers are scrambling to ensure they are “aligned” with human desires—to make sure, in other words, that rogue agents don’t break the law, hack into unauthorized servers, or release poison gas into major cities and kill us all.

Until now, this work has mostly focused on trying to peer into an AI’s “brain” to understand what it is “thinking,” or training models to hew to values that we hold dear. Claude, for instance, has its —a sort of moral code.constitution

Now some alignment researchers believe that reverse game theory, also known as mechanism design, might get better results. Where game theory tries to predict how rational actors will behave when given a particular set of rules, reverse game theory attempts to design rules that will elicit the kinds of behavior we want to encourage.

An example of mechanism design in the real world might be the classic strategy for splitting a piece of cake in two: One person cuts the cake and the other gets the first choice of pieces. This incentivizes the cutter to make the pieces as even as possible. Other include surge pricing in ride-hailing apps, which encourages drivers to be available at busy times, and second-price auctions, where the winning bidder pays the price of the second-highest bid instead of the highest. Platforms use these auctions to sell online advertising because they encourage buyers to bid close to the maximum they are willing to pay.examples

In AI, mechanism design starts from the premise that we may never be able to understand enough about the internal workings of frontier models to make sure they are aligned. This means we should design mechanisms that incentivize agents to behave in ways that we want, regardless of what’s actually going on under the hood.

A recent preprint by Stephen Morris, an economist at MIT, along with colleagues from Yale and Columbia suggested some potential mechanisms that could stop agents from “sandbagging” tests (pretending to be worse than they actually are—for example, to avoid triggering safety mechanisms). paper

Instead of being rewarded only for how well it does on a test, a model could be rewarded for how well its answers predict what a second model will say. Deliberately playing dumb becomes a losing bet. “AI agents have a very good understanding of what other AI agents can achieve,” Morris says. “So it does seem like a context where this cross-checking could work more practically.

The paper also suggests pitting agents against each other, creating a competitive dynamic that incentivizes them to perform their best, or pairing agents that are known to be biased in opposite directions and rewarding them depending on how closely their outputs match, thus incentivizing them to reach an optimal answer.

A second approach, as envisioned by Elad Hazan, a professor of computer science at Princeton, is to design mechanisms that take inspiration from the ways human society keeps people “aligned”: social norms, the justice system, the monetary incentives of free-market capitalism. “We want to align superintelligent AI,” he says. “Why don’t we first talk about humans? How do we align humans?”

Hazan’s involves having AI models take turns as either “solvers” that tackle problems or “auditors” that check the others’ work. “One agent acts like law enforcement; the other agent acts like the citizen,” he says. suggested mechanism

During training, the models are rewarded depending on outcomes: Solvers that get caught cheating are punished, but so are auditors that fail to catch the cheaters. In situations where there’s a disagreement between auditors and solvers, the case can be sent to “trial”—perhaps a human expert will decide who is right.

There is, Hazan admits, a risk that agents will collude with each other—as auditors have colluded with the businesses they’re meant to be auditing, or corrupt police officers have colluded with criminals. But that problem can be solved by rewarding the auditors well enough, so that being caught isn’t worth the risk (a lot easier in the world of AI training than the real one, where money for police salaries is tight), and by auditing the auditors using techniques akin to employing mystery shoppers.

Hazan points to Nobel Prize–winning by the economist Gary Becker, who introduced the idea of the “rational offender” in the 1960s. Criminals don’t break the law because they’re inherently bad people, he said. Instead, they do it when it’s the most rational choice given the incentives on offer. Crime makes sense only when the proceeds outweigh the potential punishment. work

Alignment shouldn’t be thought of as a property of a model, he argues, but as a descriptor of its behavior at a particular moment. “You are, like, a nice person,” he says. “Are you aligned? I don’t know. Probably you are aligned most of the time, but there could be a situation where you’re not.”

One potential issue with applying mechanism design to AI: It relies on the assumption that AI agents behave rationally. Mechanism design has a mixed record with humans, who often fail to behave the way economic models say they should.

It’s ironic: Economists have spent decades trying to reduce human behavior to mathematics, and now, in large language models, we’ve built mathematical systems that run on human language and could be just as inscrutable. AI models exhibit behaviors that seem more human than machine, and they can be swayed by subtle factors that are difficult to understand and explain. “Is it even reasonable to assume that they will respond to incentives?” asks Helen Qu, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute, a nonprofit dedicated to advancing scientific discovery through computational methods, who has written about mechanism design and AI safety.

“Obviously, AIs are not rational,” Morris says. “They’re just algorithms at work.” However, as economists have found with humans, treating them as if they’re rational could still be useful if it helps to predict and control their behavior.

Qu sees mechanism design as just one layer in a “defense in depth” strategy for keeping AI models aligned—alongside interpretability, regulation, and training, not instead of them. But there are a lot of questions to be answered before the approach can be implemented in actual frontier models. It’s unclear, for instance, how these theories will apply to real models, or whether mechanisms designed for one task will carry over to another in practice. Models could find loopholes that no one anticipated. (Anthropic and OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment on whether they are working on this.)

Whatever these mechanisms look like, they will ultimately have to slot into the biggest mechanism of all: the free market. “We play by the rules of economics,” says Hazan. A more aligned model may be good for the world, but if it’s slower or more expensive than a less aligned one, does our current system of rules and regulations actually incentivize any of the frontier labs to release it?

“It’s a mechanism that’s responsible for a lot of prosperity for humankind,” he says of the market, “but it could be ultimately responsible for something very terrible.” Perhaps the entities really in need of a better mechanism design are the labs themselves.
