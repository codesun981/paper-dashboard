Got it, that makes sense. Number one, is the AI industry solving the wrong bottleneck? You argue we are over-investing intellectually in the supply of intelligence and under-investing in diffusion. Tell me what you mean.

If you thought about a crass metaphor of AI through electricity — there are people who generate the electrons, the power producers — for electricity to have any impact on our economy, there needed to be other people who built machine tools that run on those electrons. And that led to a profound revolution. Not only would you introduce one piece of machinery into your factory, you’d start redesigning your factories around the fact that we have electrons.SS:

Let’s call that diffusion, how does the miracle of electricity diffuse into the economy? There’s a similar question with AI. I think we are very focused on how to make better models, smarter models, more machine intelligence — and we should keep doing that — but we’re very under-indexed on how to accelerate the rate of diffusion. Where is the economic value being created? How do we turn these tokens into economic value more quickly with a great ratio around it? I’m obsessed with that, because I think there’s a license to operate in society where the benefits of this stuff diffusing really do matter and I think we’re getting the calculus around some of this pretty wrong.

So who is “we”? Who are the ones insufficiently focused on diffusion? Who are you trying to convince here?

I think the labs are, but in general the entire market structure. People are being rewarded — and it’s very rational, if you’re a lab, you’ve been in your own reinforcement-learning loop, you do fundamental research, you make a more powerful model, and your valuation doubles or quadruples and every year it seems to keep working. Baked into that is the idea that eventually this stuff will diffuse, eventually there will be economic value.SS:

But one of the conceits I’m not sure people are getting right is, what is the atomic unit of the enterprise? Let’s leave consumer out of this. The presumption, as expressed through the product strategy you see from the labs, is that the atomic unit of the enterprise is the knowledge worker — if I can make every worker more productive, that’s the ROI, and that’s not actually true. The atomic unit of the enterprise is the workflow, you can make a single supply-chain analyst more productive and generate no incremental ROI, you have to make the entire supply-chain workflow measurably more productive to capture the value.

There are some roles where the cardinality collapses — the knowledge worker is the workflow, like coding — so you have perfect product-market fit for those things, and you can chase unbounded productivity. But that’s a very small part of everything we do in the real economy.

Top Line vs. Bottom Line

Number two of the Shyam interview outline, why are ambitious customers winning? Why do some units and institutions put AI to consequential use while others struggle to move beyond experiments? How much of the difference is access to technology, and how much is leadership, urgency, authority, and an imperative to win?

This is one of the empirical things I’ve observed. It wasn’t obvious to me it would be the case, but if I segment the customers I’ve worked with — the ones who want to use AI to reduce headcount, to take costs out of the business — almost all of them have failed at even achieving those goals.SS:

If you compare it to the customers succeeding wildly, have real ambition, “I want to use AI to dominate my market”, “I want to use AI to put my competitors out of business”, “I want to use AI to do work that was always beyond the efficient frontier — things I always wanted to do but could never afford to do”. I think the conclusion I’ve come to is that there’s insufficient stimulus to motivate you to do the hard things when you have a myopic goal like getting rid of humans. There might be other factors too — human intelligence and machine intelligence aren’t exactly the same thing.

How so? How are they different?

They don’t do exactly the same things in the context of the workflow. If you spend your time trying to delete the human, by definition the AI would have to be able to do everything the human does — even if it only does 98%, you’ll fail at the specific goal.SS:

Another way to reframe it, “I have this miracle intelligence now, how do I build the workflow around what it can do, rather than shoehorning it into the error that always exists between the human and the AI?” — the AI maximalist would say, “I’m just going to keep training a better model”.

But I’d say, what percentage of the problems we have today can we solve without going beyond the current frontier? I think we’ve left a lot of problems unsolved because we’re not thinking about diffusion enough, and wer’e not actually trying to maximize the AI for what it can do, we’re instead trying to judge AI by the standard of, “How do I eliminate humans?”, which may not even be that valuable, judging by the people who are succeeding with it.

Does this lesson of the importance and power that comes from focusing on the top line versus the bottom line — was that a lesson you learned in the age of AI, or something you learned working with companies before current LLMs came along?

It’s very pronounced in the age of AI. Before then, you’d say it’s more exciting and you succeed more often going after the top line, but you’ll still succeed going after OpEx reduction. With AI, the outcomes are much more binary. It’s going to lead to a category of winners and a category of people who view this as something that’s not quite working.SS:
