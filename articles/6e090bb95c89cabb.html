Slush 2026 brings a smaller, sharper bet on European startups to Helsinki

ComplexDiscovery Staff Finland marks the International Day for Failure today, a custom that began in 2010 when a student group at Aalto University proposed celebrating failure instead of lamenting it, Yle reported. Five weeks from now, a startup event that Aalto says its student community helped spark will test how far that appetite for risk can carry European technology. Slush 2026 runs Nov. 18 and 19 at Messukeskus, the Helsinki Expo and Convention Centre, with Investor Day and a founders' program on Nov. 17. The organizers have branded this edition a bet on Europe. The program published so far pairs that slogan with a sitting head of state, a European commissioner, a deliberately smaller crowd and a rebuilt startup competition. ComplexDiscovery will attend and report from Slush for the second consecutive year.

A smaller crowd, by design

Behind that program sits an unusual organizer. Slush is a not-for-profit owned by the Startup Foundation, a Finnish foundation that receives most of any profit the event makes, according to the organization's own description. It began in 2008 as a 250-person gathering. Its 2026 FAQ projects 5,800 startups and 3,300 investors, and its homepage advertises 12,000-plus attendees, 200-plus speakers and 600-plus side events across what Slush calls Slush Week. Students and recent graduates run the operation, supported by a volunteer corps that Slush's own pages put at between 1,600 and 1,800 people. Selectivity runs through the whole event. Slush says it does not pay speakers and speakers do not pay it, and every company seeking to attend must complete an application that the Slush team reviews personally. This year the headcount is smaller on purpose. When Slush named Noora Saksa chief executive on Jan. 27, it said the 2026 event would cut its audience by 10 percent to better serve its core of early-stage startups. Personal recommendations will tailor the experience to each company's growth stage, the announcement said. Saksa, who first joined Slush as a volunteer in 2021, framed the stakes in economic terms. "Startups are our best bet for GDP growth in Europe," she said in the announcement. She also set a goal there of at least two European technology companies in the global top 10 by market capitalization by 2050.

Announcements that set the 2026 program

The same rethinking reached Slush 100, the pitch competition whose earlier formats date to the event's 2008 founding year. This year's winner receives €500,000 with no equity taken, replacing the €1 million investment from venture firms that winners received from 2022 through 2025, according to Slush. "A flat €1M at a fixed valuation and standard terms no longer fits most startups," Saksa said. Dropping the investor-led model also opens the contest to founders from outside Europe and the U.S., Slush said. From over 1,400 applicants, Slush named 100 companies and then 20 semifinalists, who pitch on the Startup Stage on the first morning before three finalists compete for the prize. Slush also created Slush 20 Under 20, a program for builders who have not yet turned 20. The first list, published this month, names 20 people from over a dozen countries, the youngest of them 15, who together have raised over €10 million from venture investors, according to Slush. Anthropic, Index Ventures and Sifted support the program. "Europe's first €1T company could be founded by a teenager," Saksa said. Off the stage, Slush has changed address. Since July, it has worked from Kasvutalo, or Growth House, in Helsinki's Kamppi district. That is the new name Slush and the startup community Founders House gave to Sähkötalo, an Alvar Aalto building completed in 1973 as head office of the city's electricity utility. A Sept. 2 release counts about 50 companies and communities in the building, around 30 of them startups, investors and startup communities. "Every leading tech hub in Europe has its own startup district," Saksa said in the release.

Who is on stage, and why practitioners should listen

Those year-round moves feed a November program whose speakers are now partly public. Slush has published what it calls its first batch of 2026 speakers and says the stage program is full. The roster includes two officeholders, Alexander Stubb, Finland's 13th president, and Ekaterina Zaharieva, the European commissioner for startups, research and innovation. It also features Jessica Livingston, co-founder of Y Combinator; David Silver, former lead of DeepMind's reinforcement learning team and now chief executive of Ineffable Intelligence; and Matthew Prince, co-founder and chief executive of Cloudflare. Beyond Prince, cybersecurity professionals will find two security-company founders on the list. Eoin Hinchy ran security operations for 15 years at companies including eBay, PayPal and DocuSign before co-founding Tines, according to his Slush biography, and Madeline Lawrence is co-founder of Aikido Security. Legal technology arrives through Ross McNairn, whose company Wordsmith builds AI software for corporate legal departments. Paolo Fois of Lexroom is also listed, and his Slush biography says over 15 percent of Italian legal proceedings pass through Lexroom, without explaining how that share is measured. The third legal name is Joshua Browder of DoNotPay, whose speaker biography describes the service as "the world's first robot lawyer." That phrase has a regulatory history. In a September 2024 complaint, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) alleged that DoNotPay did not test whether its AI lawyer service performed at the level of a human lawyer. The commission voted 5-0 on Jan. 16, 2025, to approve a final order, which the agency announced Feb. 11. The order requires DoNotPay to pay $193,000 and bars it from claiming its service performs like a real lawyer without sufficient evidence to back that up, according to the FTC. For eDiscovery and information governance teams weighing AI vendors, the lesson reaches beyond one company, because a federal regulator has already required one legal AI provider to support its performance claims with evidence. Every vendor claim heard in Helsinki deserves the same scrutiny. Company law has its own advocates on the program. Simon Schaefer, Iwona Anna Biernat and Vojtech Horna appear for EU-INC, a movement Schaefer co-founded in late 2024, according to his Slush biography. The European Commission has since proposed an EU-level company form under the EU Inc. name, which it describes as optional and digital by default. In its March 18 release presenting the proposed regulation, the Commission called on the European Parliament and the Council to agree on it by the end of 2026. In Parliament, the file has hit a delay: a vote in the legal affairs committee planned for Oct. 8 was postponed, with a new date around Oct. 19, Agence Europe and Table.Briefings reported. Attendees planning their days should know that Slush's public pages, as reviewed Oct. 13, list speakers and opening hours but not a session-by-session schedule. The media page says the agenda launches in the fall, and for 2025 Slush published its full stage agenda on the Slush Platform, the same online tool attendees use to book meetings. The main event opens with the Slush 2026 Opening Show at 10 a.m. Nov. 18.

What Slush's own research claims, and what it cannot

Whatever the final schedule, Slush's sales pitch rests on research it publishes itself. The Slush Startup Report 2026, released in May with data partner Dealroom, compared 2,108 European companies that first attended Slush in 2022, 2023 or 2024 with 229,866 European peers matched on founding year, country and growth stage. Every company in the Slush group was founded in 2016 or later and had not reached a Series B round when it first attended. After that first attendance, 26.4 percent of the Slush companies raised institutional capital, against 7.5 percent of benchmark peers measured over what the report calls the equivalent post-2022 period, which the report presents as a 3.5-times premium. On investor quality, 5.6 percent of the Slush companies were backed by a fund in the top 0.1 percent of Dealroom's global investor ranking, against 0.8 percent of peers. Readers should weigh those figures against the report's own caveats. The two groups start in different places. Nearly half the Slush companies, 48 percent, had no institutional backing when they first attended, while 89 percent of the benchmark companies were bootstrapped. The report acknowledges that already-backed companies raise money at higher rates in both groups, which is why its aggregate premium exceeds the premium within either subgroup. Among companies with no outside funding at first attendance, 17 percent of the Slush group raised venture capital afterward, against 5.6 percent of peers. Among those already backed, the rates were 35 percent and 23.2 percent. Its methodology also concedes that matching cannot account for how ambitious, well connected or ready to raise the attending companies already were. The findings are correlational, the report says, and its matching "cannot separate who attends from what attending does." The report closes by telling readers the data suggests the trip is worth taking, which marks it as research produced by the organization that sells the tickets. Two other Slush studies frame this year's message. In a February survey of 310 Finnish founders and operators, 53 percent of respondents said they were considering or actively planning to move business operations abroad, and 56 percent named general attitudes and culture as obstacles to growth. Slush says the respondents represent about 10 percent of Finland's active startups. It sent 10 reform proposals to Prime Minister Petteri Orpo, who said his government would review them in its April budget framework discussions. An October 2025 study with Dealroom and Accel found that 68 of 459 former full-time Slush staff members, or 15 percent, had gone on to found companies.

Finland's stage carries geopolitical weight

Slush makes its case for European founders from a city whose venues carry their own history. Investor Day on Nov. 17 takes place at Alvar Aalto's Finlandia Hall on Töölö Bay, where Slush's Investor Day page promises over 2,000 venture investors. Leaders of 35 states gathered there for the 1975 Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe. They signed the Helsinki Final Act on the summit's last day, Aug. 1, 1975, according to the Finnish Heritage Agency and Switzerland's National Museum. Finland's own position has shifted since then. It became NATO's 31st member on April 4, 2023, and Stubb took office as president on March 1, 2024. Helsinki also hosts the European Centre of Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats, which counts 35 participating states, all of them members of the EU or NATO. For security teams with Finnish operations, the country's Cybersecurity Act, which implements the EU's NIS 2 Directive, began phasing in obligations for covered entities on April 8, 2025, according to Traficom. Those entities then had until May 8 of that year to register and until July 8 to establish risk-management procedures, although public administration entities received no separate transition period for that risk-management obligation, the agency said. The technology record behind the event runs deep. Linus Torvalds, who enrolled at the University of Helsinki in 1988, started work on the kernel later named Linux in the fall of 1991, according to the Computer History Museum. Satellite company ICEYE, whose co-founder Rafal Modrzewski is on the 2026 speaker list, launched its technology in Finland and now serves defense clients worldwide, according to Slush's Helsinki page.

What ComplexDiscovery will be watching

ComplexDiscovery's coverage from Helsinki last year followed similar threads, examining how Harvey scaled domain-specific legal AI, how the BORDERLAND gathering approached dual-use defense technology, and what data provenance meant for information governance teams. Four threads stand out for 2026. The first is evidence: when legal AI founders cite adoption or accuracy figures on stage, record the claim and its basis, because statements made on stage can resurface in sales materials and procurement files. The second is corporate form: if EU Inc. advances, counterparties incorporated under a new EU-level form are likely to raise registry and records questions for diligence and discovery teams, in this publication's assessment. The third is resilience, because defense, space and infrastructure companies on the program, ICEYE among them, work in sectors where cybersecurity, export controls and data sovereignty overlap.

The fourth is timing. Two of the 20 Slush 100 semifinalists build products around sensitive information: Foundation Clinic, which Slush lists as "the health-data platform for European healthcare," and GraceX, pitched as "your company's brain" with privacy built in. In this publication's assessment, companies at that stage are where security, information governance and discovery needs first take shape, which makes Slush a place for providers in those fields to see what early-stage clients will ask of them.

The International Day for Failure, as Yle describes it, aims to take the stigma out of mistakes so that people feel free to create and innovate. Slush is now asking Europe to place a larger bet on its own builders, while its own research concedes it cannot separate the founders who walk in from what the room itself does for them. Which claims made on Helsinki's stages next month will your organization be ready to verify before it relies on them?

  • Chatham House's FAQ, in an answer about photographs taken outside the room, says people may repeat what was said so long as they do not identify the speaker or another participant "directly or indirectly." Can that standard be met for a panel whose members are named in advance?
  • Chatham House holds every session of its Competition Policy Conference 2026 under the Rule and names the speakers on the conference page. Does that settle the question, or does it show only that an organizer's advance publicity falls outside what the Rule binds?
  • The same agenda names a single keynote speaker. What deniability does the Rule leave a speaker who is alone on the program?
  • The FAQ allows a single conference session to be designated under the Rule. Does it follow that the session's roster should be kept off the published agenda?
  • Another FAQ answer lets a speaker say afterward that he or she was there and what he or she said. Does the Rule treat presence as protected, or only attribution?
  • Is the narrower convention many organizers seem to intend (named panelists, no remark tied to any one of them) better described as "not for attribution"?
  • How much protection does one-in-three deniability give a speaker, given that the Rule does not change what a speaker is permitted to disclose?
  • What should a press policy say when an event publishes its roster and invokes the Rule?

An invitation

The tutorial ends without a tidy verdict, and that is by design. Each proposition survives in a narrower form than it began with, and each narrowing is where the useful thinking happened. Treat it as an invitation to argue the side you do not believe: that the Chatham House Rule cannot survive the recorded room, that anonymity at industry events is a privilege readers pay for, and that event reporting should be on the record by default. Then argue the opposite of each, and take the collections question on the published speaker list cold, without a model answer. The questions are free. The thinking is the work.

Is the Chatham House Rule outdated? An Oxford-style tutorial for event reporting

ComplexDiscovery Staff The Chatham House Rule fits in a single sentence. Participants may use what they hear at a meeting, but may not reveal the identity or affiliation of a speaker or of any other participant. ComplexDiscovery OÜ has adapted a traditional, essay-based form of the Oxford tutorial, in which an argument is defended under close questioning, to test that sentence against modern industry events. Those events are where cybersecurity, information governance and eDiscovery professionals compare notes, in a field that often moves faster than it reflects. The test is practical for any event that announces the Rule for an entire program after publishing its panelists' names, titles and employers. Such rooms can be large and streamed, and AI meeting assistants are available on the major platforms used to stream them. Chatham House, which created the Rule in 1927 and refined it in 1992 and 2002, answers "No" in its frequently asked questions to recording and to AI notetakers under the Rule. It also says it uses the Rule "not often" for its own large events. Yet the institute's own calendar complicates that picture. Every session of its Competition Policy Conference 2026, set for Nov. 17 in London, is held under the Rule, and the conference page names the speakers and their affiliations. The tutorial treats that choice as the strongest counterargument to its own propositions and returns to it in every term.

What it is

That tension is what the format is built to examine. The ComplexDiscovery Tutorial on the Chatham House Rule and event reporting sets three contestable propositions across three terms, one per term, and pairs each with a worked example: a model essay and a tutor's interrogation of it. Readers watch the method at work rather than take a conclusion on faith. A closing collections question, set without a model answer, asks whether a published speaker list can coexist with the Rule at all. Each proposition is written to be argued either way, and none represents ComplexDiscovery OÜ's editorial position.

An argument under questioning

The Oxford tutorial behind that format is less a lecture than a disciplined conversation. This package adapts one traditional, essay-based version of it, in which a student writes an essay, reads it aloud and then defends it while a tutor presses on its weakest joints. Each essay here takes a position, marshals evidence and anticipates objections in roughly 900 words. The point is not to win the hour but to learn which parts of an argument survive pressure and which only sounded persuasive on the page.

A method, then three terms

Across the package's three terms, each argument builds on the last, moving from whether the Rule can be enforced, to who bears the cost of the anonymity it grants, to what practice should replace its blanket use. The sections take their names from Oxford's three academic terms, which the university labels autumn, spring and summer: Michaelmas (October to December), Hilary (January to March) and Trinity (April to June), according to its dates of term.

Michaelmas: Can the Rule be enforced?

The first proposition holds that the Chatham House Rule cannot survive the recorded room. Its readings start with the Rule's text and FAQ, which rule out recording and AI notetakers under the Rule. In an answer about photographs taken outside the room, the FAQ also says people may repeat what was said so long as they do not identify the speaker or another participant "directly or indirectly." That phrase is where the term's argument turns. Latanya Sweeney's 2000 working paper, "Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely," shows how quickly description becomes identification. Using 1990 census data, she estimated that 87 percent of the U.S. population could likely be uniquely identified by five-digit ZIP code, gender and date of birth. Helen Nissenbaum's "Privacy as Contextual Integrity" (2004) supplies the theory, treating privacy as a question of whether information flows match the norms of the context in which they began. The term asks whether the bar on indirect identification can be met once an organizer has published who is on each panel, and whether any failure belongs to the Rule or to the organizer. Chatham House's own named roster for a conference held entirely under the Rule is the counterweight the term must reckon with.

Hilary: Who pays for anonymity?

If the first term asks whether the Rule can protect speakers, the second asks what that protection costs readers. Its proposition holds that at an industry event, anonymity is a privilege the speaker enjoys and the reader pays for. The readings set journalism's own sourcing standards against the Rule. The Associated Press's News Values and Principles allow material from anonymous sources only when three conditions hold. The material must be information rather than opinion or speculation and vital to the report; it must be unavailable except under anonymity; and the source must be reliable and in a position to have direct knowledge. The Society of Professional Journalists Code of Ethics, revised Oct. 4, 2026, tells journalists to reserve anonymity as a last resort for sources with information of substantial public interest who may face danger, retribution or other harm.
